ABSTRACT 
This report describes the construction of a credit-risk scoring workflow using the Home 
Credit Default Risk dataset available on Kaggle. The dataset is provided as seven 
interconnected tables: current applications, credit-bureau records, previous loans, 
instalment payments, point-of-sale balances, credit-card balances, and monthly bureau 
balances. These tables were aggregated into a single row per applicant, yielding 301 
predictors that capture each individual's profile, repayment history, and the external 
credit scores included with the data. 
Four models were compared: two logistic regressions and two LightGBM models. An 
isotonic calibration step is applied on top of the second LightGBM model. On a held-
out test set comprising 7,500 applicants, the calibrated LightGBM model achieved an 
AUC of 0.758 and a KS of 0.375. With the threshold set at 0.05, which maximises F1, 
the model identifies 86.2% of defaulters. 
The calibrated probabilities were subsequently converted into a score, anchored at 600 
points with 20 points assigned for each doubling of the odds. Applicants were allocated 
to five rating bands. Observed default rates rise consistently across these bands, from 
1.92% in band A to 30.04% in band E. 
The entire analysis can be reproduced from a single notebook, and the final model is 
deployed via a Streamlit application packaged as a Docker image. One limitation should 
be noted: only a 12,750-row subsample was available, so the results are subject to 
considerable sampling uncertainty (discussed in Chapter 4). Training on the complete 
dataset would likely yield improved results. 
Keywords: credit risk, probability of default, scorecard, gradient boosting, probability 
calibration, Home Credit, LightGBM, KS statistic.

CHAPTER 1: QUESTION AND SCOPE 
1.1 Research question 
For loan applicants within the Home Credit portfolio (Population), does a gradient-
boosted decision-tree ensemble with isotonic calibration (Intervention) yield higher 
ranking quality and better-calibrated probability-of-default estimates than a regularised 
logistic-regression baseline (Comparator), as assessed by AUC, KS, Brier score and 
recall at a fixed operating threshold (Outcome), when both models are trained and 
evaluated on the same engineered feature set and the same stratified held-out test fold 
(Time / Setting)? 
1.2. Rationale for Topic Selection 
Home Credit serves borrowers that mainstream banks usually turn down—people in 
emerging markets with little or no formal credit history. For these applicants, a 
traditional bureau score is either missing or of limited value. Estimating the probability 
of default (PD) thus becomes the core underwriting task. Basel's internal-ratings-based 
approach also demands that PD estimates be both discriminating and well calibrated, 
since expected loss equals PD times LGD times EAD. A model that ranks applicants 
well but produces inconsistent probabilities is of little use for pricing or provisioning. 
This project tackles exactly that dual requirement using a public, richly structured data 
set. 
 
Figure 1. Home Credit business overview. 
1.3 Scope 
All experiments rely on a stratified subsample of 12,750 applicants taken from the full 
307,511-row extract (random_state = 42), keeping computation feasible on a local 
machine. Performance on the full data would likely be higher, and subsample results 
carry considerable sampling uncertainty (Chapter 4). The study addresses PD estimation

1.4 Brief related work 
Logistic regression has long been the industry standard in credit scoring, as it is 
interpretable and converts easily into points-based scorecards (Hand &amp; Henley, 
1997; Siddiqi, 2006). Benchmarking studies show ensemble methods often outperform 
single classifiers, yet logistic regression remains a strong, transparent baseline 
(Lessmann et al., 2015). Gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) and its efficient 
implementation LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) are now staples of tabular risk modelling. 
For probability calibration, Platt scaling (Platt, 1999) and isotonic regression (Zadrozny 
&amp; Elkan, 2002) are the most common post-hoc corrections, and non-parametric 
methods need a reasonably large hold-out set to stay reliable (Niculescu-Mizil &amp; 
Caruana, 2005) — directly relevant to the modest validation fold used here.

CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY 
3.1. Pipeline Overview 
The workflow has four stages. In the first, the six auxiliary tables are summarised to one 
row per applicant and merged with the application table on SK_ID_CURR. In the 
second, the combined table is cleaned and enriched with engineered features. In the 
third, models are trained, tuned and compared. In the fourth, the selected model is 
calibrated, a threshold is chosen, scores and bands are constructed, and the result is 
exposed through a web interface. Figure 6 outlines the preprocessing stage and Figure 
7 the modelling stage.

3.2. Data Partitioning 
The sample is divided into training, validation and test folds, stratified on the target so 
that each fold keeps the same default rate of about 8% (random_state = 42). Each fold 
has a distinct role. The training fold is used to fit the models. The validation fold is used 
for early stopping, calibration and threshold selection. The test fold is held back until 
the very end, so that the metrics reported in Chapter 4 reflect performance on data that 
played no part in any modelling decision. Table 2 gives the size of each fold.

3.3. Preprocessing and feature engineering 
Preprocessing relies on a single scikit-learn ColumnTransformer (Pedregosa et al., 2011), fitted 
only on the training fold and applied unchanged to the validation and test folds, keeping steps 
consistent across models and preventing leakage. Numeric variables are median-imputed and 
standardised. Missing values in categorical variables are replaced with a dedicated MISSING 
level, and the variables are one-hot encoded with the first level dropped. Sentinel values are 
handled before imputation: 365,243 in DAYS_EMPLOYED becomes missing plus a binary 
flag, the gender code XNA becomes missing, and OWN_CAR_AGE is set to missing for 
applicants without a car. 
In total, 301 features are built in four blocks (Table 3). Time variables are converted from days 
to years, and ratio features relate the loan to the applicant's means. The three external scores are 
summarised by their mean, minimum, maximum, standard deviation, product and count. Rather 
than imputing the external scores aggressively, the pipeline records how many are present 
(EXT_SOURCE_COUNT), since having an external score is itself informative and filling the 
gap with a mean or median would erase that signal. Auxiliary tables are aggregated with a 
groupby on SK_ID_CURR, and applicants with no bureau record are flagged 
(HAS_BUREAU).

3.5. Calibration and Threshold Selection 
Calibration matters whenever downstream decisions depend on absolute PD values. An 
isotonic regression is fitted on the validation fold to map the raw LightGBM output to a 
calibrated probability. The threshold is then chosen on the same fold using the rule in 
Equation (7), which gives τ = 0.05. On the test fold this threshold recovers 86.2% of 
defaulters. Because the validation fold is used for early stopping, calibration and 
threshold choice alike, any optimism in those three steps would show up as a drop 
between validation and test; the test fold is the only fair reference. 
3.6. Scorecard Construction 
Calibrated probabilities are mapped to points using Equation (4), with a base score of 
600, a PDO of 20 and base odds of 50:1, following the standard scorecard convention 
(Siddiqi, 2006). Applicants are then assigned to five rating bands, A to E, from lowest 
to highest risk. The bands are evaluated not by how they look but by whether the 
observed default rate rises steadily from A to E on the test fold.

The class-balanced logistic regression deserves a comment. Its recall of 1.0000 together 
with a precision of 0.0831, almost identical to the 8.0% base rate, shows that at its 
operating threshold it flags virtually every applicant, which is useless as a decision rule. 
Its Brier score of 0.1940 is also about three times larger than the others, because re-
weighting the classes pushes the predicted probabilities far above the true default 
frequency. The case illustrates why class weights are a poor remedy when calibrated 
probabilities are needed. 
Calibration changed the picture slightly. The calibrated LightGBM gives up 0.0012 of 
AUC and about 0.011 of KS relative to the raw model, and its Brier score is marginally 
worse (0.0678 against 0.0677), so on this test fold the calibration step did not measurably 
improve the probability error. It did, however, move the operating point to a higher recall 
(86.2% against 83.8%) and, as Section 4.2 shows, bring predicted probabilities closer to 
observed frequencies over most of the score range. The calibrated model is retained for 
production because the scorecard in Section 4.4 requires probabilities rather than bare 
scores, and because the loss in ranking quality is small. 
4.2. Evaluation Curves 
Figures 8 to 11 show the ROC curves, the precision–recall curves, the KS plot and the 
reliability diagram. The ROC curves lie well above the diagonal. The precision–recall 
curves show that, at low and moderate recall, precision is several times the base rate of 
0.08, although it falls steadily as recall rises, which is the usual price of pushing a model 
towards catching nearly every defaulter. The KS statistic of the calibrated model peaks 
at 0.375, comfortably above the informal 0.30 benchmark mentioned in Section 2.6. The 
reliability diagram indicates that calibrated probabilities track observed default 
frequencies closely across most of the range, with mild over-confidence between 0.1 
and 0.3, an artefact that isotonic regression sometimes produces when the calibration set 
is small.

4.5. Business Impact with prediction 
At τ = 0.05, the model flags 4,057 of 7,500 test applicants (54%) for review. Of these, 
520 are true defaulters, so the model catches 86.2% of the 603 defaulters in the test fold 
at 12.8% precision—roughly 1.6 times the base rate. The remaining 3,537 flagged

That is the price of the high recall target: instead of rejecting these applicants, the model 
routes them to manual review or risk-based pricing rather than automatic approval.

and E, hold just 22.7% of applicants but 55.4% of all defaulters. Including band C 
captures 73.3% of defaulters while covering 39.2% of applicants. Band A, by contrast, 
holds nearly 30% of applicants but only 7.0% of defaulters, with a lift of 0.24—a natural 
candidate for streamlined approval

4.4 Conclusion 
The pipeline works end to end, from seven raw tables to a calibrated PD model, a points-
based scorecard and five risk bands. LightGBM outperformed the logistic baseline, but 
by a modest margin (AUC 0.758 against 0.745) that is comparable to sampling 
uncertainty at this sample size. Isotonic calibration made the probabilities usable for the 
scorecard and raised recall to 86.2% at τ = 0.05, at the cost of a slight loss in 
discrimination and no gain in Brier score. The bands separate risk monotonically, from 
a 1.92% default rate in band A to 30.04% in band E. The main limitation is the 
subsample: training on the full 307,511 rows, bootstrapping the test metrics and adding 
local explanations such as SHAP are the natural next steps.

Appendix B -Cleaning log 
• DAYS_EMPLOYED sentinel 365 243 → NaN before imputation. 
• Drop columns with > 60 % missing (certain housing flags; OWN_CAR_AGE when 
the applicant owns no car). 
• Categorical NaN → MISSING bucket, then one-hot encoded (first level dropped). 
• Numeric NaN → median per column (training fold only), then standard-scaled. 
• Outliers in DAYS_BIRTH and DAYS_EMPLOYED are retained; the tree-based 
model is insensitive to monotone transforms. 
• Aggregate features computed with pandas groupby on SK_ID_CURR; no per-row 
outlier removal. 
Appendix C -Assumptions register 
• A1. The TARGET column is observed at the loan horizon; right-censoring is 
negligible for this portfolio. 
• A2. Applicants in the test fold are exchangeable with future applicants in the next 
quarter (no regime change). 
• A3. External credit scores (EXT_SOURCE_*) are point-in-time and free of leakage. 
• A4. Behavioural-history aggregates are computable for new applicants at decision 
time (no look-ahead bias).
